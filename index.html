Unnecessary Stuff... But Fun!

Personal quirks and legendary items left in the dust to catch the eye.

Apollo 14 (1971)

Alan Shepard's 2 Golf Balls

Snuck aboard in a sock and attached to a soil sampling tool. Shepard struck two golf balls in lunar low gravity, watching them sail "miles and miles and miles" across the horizon.

Location: Fra Mauro Fun Physics Experiment
Apollo 15 (1971)

Falcon Feather & Steel Hammer

Astronaut David Scott dropped a 1.3kg aluminum hammer and a 30g falcon feather on live television to prove Galileo's theory: in a vacuum, all objects fall at the exact same rate.

Location: Hadley-Apennine Galilean Gravity Test
Apollo 16 (1972)

Charlie Duke's Family Photo

A framed photograph of astronaut Charlie Duke, his wife Dotty, and their sons Charles and Tom, shrink-wrapped and placed on the soil—an emotional human connection to an explorer's home.

Location: Descartes Highlands Personal Monument
Apollo 15 (1971)

"Fallen Astronaut" Figurine

A tiny 3.5-inch aluminum sculpture created by Belgian artist Paul Van Hoeydonck. It rests next to a plaque listing 14 astronauts and cosmonauts who died in the pursuit of space exploration.

Location: Hadley Rille Memorial Sculpture

Apollo Debrief & FAQ

Answering the essential curiosity questions about the Apollo lunar program.

NASA named the lunar program after Apollo, the ancient Greek god of light, music, poetry, and—most fittingly—the sun and archery (symbolizing hitting a distant target).

NASA administrator Abe Silverstein suggested the name in 1960 because he felt the image of the god Apollo riding his chariot across the sun was a grand, fitting metaphor for a massive voyage traveling across space to the Moon.

No, not every Apollo mission was crewed.

  • Uncrewed Missions: Early flights like Apollo 4, Apollo 5, and Apollo 6 were completely uncrewed robotic tests. They were designed to test the massive Saturn V rocket and the Lunar Module in space before risking human lives.
  • The Tragedy of Apollo 1: Apollo 1 was meant to be the first crewed flight, but a tragic cabin fire on the launch pad on January 27, 1967, killed all three astronauts inside (Gus Grissom, Ed White, and Roger B. Chaffee). (It was originally designated AS-204, but renamed Apollo 1 in their honor).
  • Crewed Missions: Crewed flights officially ran from Apollo 7 through Apollo 17.

While the mission numbers go up to 17, the exact count depends on how you tally them because of testing phases and skipped numbers:

  • The Number Sequence: The numbered sequence officially runs from Apollo 1 to Apollo 17, but there were no missions named Apollo 2 or Apollo 3 (those designations were dropped during early uncrewed suborbital tests).
  • Total Dedicated Flights: In total, NASA flew 15 distinct missions under the Apollo name (3 uncrewed test flights and 11 crewed missions).
  • Moon Landings: Out of the crewed flights, 6 missions successfully landed humans on the Moon (Apollo 11, 12, 14, 15, 16, and 17), allowing 12 American astronauts to walk on the lunar surface. (Apollo 13 suffered an oxygen tank explosion en route and had to loop back around the Moon safely without landing).
